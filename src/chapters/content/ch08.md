Two functions could give the same partition in Chapter 7 because we kept only which inputs belonged together. The same decision comes up when we draw objects from an urn. Write down what we keep from one draw. If two draws give the same record, they count once.

## 8.1 The Four Urn Cases

Suppose we draw two numbers from $[3]$. Is $(1,2)$ different from $(2,1)$? Is $(1,1)$ allowed? These are decisions about what the answer records. The answer cannot be a formula until we know what counts as one outcome.

The urn notation records those two decisions. $+M$ allows repeated choices and $-M$ forbids them. $+A$ distinguishes arrangements and $-A$ forgets their order. To draw $k$ objects from $[n]$, use this translation.

| Case | An outcome is | Number of outcomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $+M+A$ | A tuple in $[n]^k$ | $n^k$ |
| $-M+A$ | An injective tuple | $n!/(n-k)!$ for $k\le n$ |
| $-M-A$ | A $k$-element subset | $\binom nk$ |
| $+M-A$ | A multiset of size $k$ | $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ for $n\ge1$ |

With order and repetition, each slot still has $n$ choices after all earlier choices have been made. Without repetition, the choices decrease by one at every step. For $0\le k\le n$, their product is $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=n!/(n-k)!$. If $k>n$, the injective case gives zero. The empty draw is one outcome, so $0!=1$ fits the counting argument, including the case of a permutation of no objects.

Now forget order in the injective case. Each subset of size $k$ gives exactly $k!$ distinct ordered tuples, one for each way to arrange its elements. Thus

$$
\left|\binom Ak\right|=\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\qquad(|A|=n,\ 0\le k\le n).
$$

The notation $\binom Ak$ names the **set** of subsets. $\binom nk$ names the **number** of them. Choosing the complement gives $\binom nk=\binom n{n-k}$. For $n\ge1$, fix one distinguished element. Once we decide to include it, there are $k-1$ places left to fill. If we leave it out, there are still $k$ places to fill. Both choices draw from the same $n-1$ remaining elements. Adding these disjoint cases gives Pascal’s recursion, with $\binom nk=0$ outside $0\le k\le n$:

$$
\binom nk=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}k.
$$

## 8.2 Multisets and Separators

The unordered draws $1,1,3$ and $1,3,1$ give the same multiset $\{1,1,3\}_M$. It records two copies of 1, none of 2, and one of 3. A multiset over $[n]$ is encoded by its multiplicities $(m_1,\ldots,m_n)$, with $m_i\in\mathbb N_0$. Its size is $\sum_i m_i$. Each label still has a position in the multiplicity tuple, even though the original draws no longer have an order.

Put three stars into three labeled groups using two separators. Read $\star\star\,|\,|\,\star$ from left to right. Two stars before the first bar give $m_1=2$. The gap between the bars gives $m_2=0$. The last star gives $m_3=1$. Empty groups still get a place, even at the beginning or end. Given the multiplicities, write that many stars for each group and one bar between consecutive groups. This reverses the reading exactly. Choosing the positions of the $k$ stars among $k+n-1$ symbols thus gives

$$
\left|\{(m_1,\ldots,m_n)\in\mathbb N_0^n:\ \textstyle\sum_i m_i=k\}\right|=\binom{k+n-1}{k}.
$$

::: manim ch08_multisets.mp4 | Read the spaces between separators
Move the dividers and compare the resulting multiplicities. An empty group still occupies a position in the tuple.
:::

With two draws, the multiset $\{1,1\}_M$ has one arrangement and $\{1,2\}_M$ has two. More generally, a fixed multiset of size $k$ has $k!/(m_1!\cdots m_n!)$ arrangements. The denominator is justified because permuting positions occupied by the same label leaves the word unchanged.

If each $m_i$ has to be positive, put one star in every group first and distribute $k-n$ remaining stars. For $k\ge n$ this gives $\binom{k-1}{n-1}$. For an **at most** budget, add a final group for the amount left unspent. It transforms an inequality into an exact sum with a reversible step.

::: practice Use the unspent budget
Distribute at most 8 indivisible units among three labeled recipients, giving each at least 1. Count the distributions.
::: answer
Set aside one unit for each recipient, leaving up to 5 units. If the recipients use 4 of those units, the amount left over has to be 1. Every split forces its leftover in the same way. So we can count an exact sum of 5 with four nonnegative entries, three extras and the leftover. The number is $\binom{5+4-1}{4-1}=\binom83$.
:::

### Per-coordinate upper caps and monotone grid paths

When each coordinate also has an **upper cap** $0\le m_i\le c$ in $m_1+\cdots+m_n=k$ (for instance, counting $n$-digit decimal strings with digit sum $k$, where $c=9$), combine stars-and-bars with inclusion–exclusion over the bad events $B_i=\{m_i\ge c+1\}$. Violating $s$ chosen caps at once pre-allocates $s(c+1)$ stars to those coordinates and distributes the remaining $k-s(c+1)$ stars freely, giving
$$
\sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor k/(c+1)\rfloor}(-1)^s\binom ns\binom{k-s(c+1)+n-1}{n-1}.
$$

A closely related model counts **monotone lattice paths** from $(0,0)$ to $(W,H)$ using only right steps $(1,0)$ and up steps $(0,1)$. Every path is a word of $W$ right steps and $H$ up steps, so choosing which $W$ of the $W+H$ steps move right gives $\binom{W+H}{W}$ paths. To avoid a **forbidden point** $(x,y)$, subtract the paths that pass through $(x,y)$—each splits uniquely into a path from $(0,0)$ to $(x,y)$ followed by one from $(x,y)$ to $(W,H)$, leaving $\binom{W+H}{W}-\binom{x+y}{x}\binom{(W-x)+(H-y)}{W-x}$. For two forbidden points lying on a monotone chain $(0,0)\preceq P_1\preceq P_2\preceq(W,H)$, add back the three-leg paths visiting both $P_1$ and $P_2$ by inclusion–exclusion.

## 8.3 Binomial and Multinomial Coefficients

In $(x+y)^4$, each of the four factors contributes either an $x$ or a $y$. To obtain $x^2y^2$, decide which two factors supply $x$. This gives six ways. Here multiplication commutes, so choosing factors 1 and 3 gives $xyxy$ and choosing factors 1 and 2 gives $xxyy$, and both products become the same monomial. The coefficient counts those choices:

$$
(x+y)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk x^k y^{n-k}.
$$

For $n=n_1+\cdots+n_k$, successively choose the positions occupied by $x_1$, then $x_2$, and so on. Cancelling factorials in the product of binomial coefficients gives

$$
\binom n{n_1,\ldots,n_k}=\frac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_k!},\qquad
(x_1+\cdots+x_k)^n=\sum_{n_1+\cdots+n_k=n}\binom n{n_1,\ldots,n_k}\prod_{i=1}^k x_i^{n_i}.
$$

This counts words with prescribed letter multiplicities, or ordered blocks of prescribed sizes using positions as the elements. For the coefficient of $x^2yz$ in $(2x+3y+5z)^4$, the positions can be chosen in $\binom4{2,1,1}=12$ ways. Each choice then contributes the same numerical factor $2^2\cdot3\cdot5=60$. The coefficient is $720$. The term count is different from the coefficient of one term. The number of possible exponent triples of total degree 4 is $\binom62$, by the separator argument.

If we keep block sizes but forget block labels, we must identify partitions that differ only by exchanging equal-sized blocks. Take the profile $(1,2,2)$ on five elements. Put the singleton in the first slot. The two pairs can go in either of the last two slots, so the multinomial coefficient has counted the same partition twice. We get $\binom5{1,2,2}/2!=15$. A factor of $3!$ would require all three slots to accept the singleton, but the fixed profile gives it only one slot.

For positive $n_i$, let $\mu_j$ be the number of blocks of size $j$. In a fixed sorted profile $(n_1,\ldots,n_k)$, the blocks of size $j$ have $\mu_j!$ ways to occupy their slots. Each unlabeled partition therefore has exactly $\prod_j\mu_j!$ representatives among the ordered partitions counted by the multinomial coefficient. Divide by that product.

::: practice Count only the degrees that appear
How many distinct monomials of degree at most 4 occur in $(1+x+y+z)^9$?
::: answer
Each $(a,b,c)$ with $a+b+c\le4$ occurs, since $9-a-b-c$ factors can supply the constant. All terms have positive coefficients. Add a slack variable so that $a+b+c+s=4$, leaving $\binom{4+4-1}{4-1}=\binom73$ choices. The slack in this count tracks unused **degree allowance**, not the number of constants in the expansion.
:::

## 8.4 Distributing Objects and Counting Partitions

Consider five tasks assigned to three employees, with every employee receiving at least one task. Model a single assignment by collecting its fibres, for example $(\{1,5\},\{3\},\{2,4\})$. Now decide what the observer can tell apart.

| Tasks | Employees | Information that remains | Count for $n$ tasks and $k$ nonempty groups |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Labeled | Labeled | A surjection, or labeled fibres | $k!S_{n,k}$ |
| Labeled | Unlabeled | A partition of the set $[n]$ | $S_{n,k}$ |
| Unlabeled | Labeled | An ordered tuple of positive loads | $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ |
| Unlabeled | Unlabeled | A sorted tuple of positive loads | $P_{n,k}$ |

With distinguishable tasks, swapping tasks 1 and 3 changes the record to $(\{3,5\},\{1\},\{2,4\})$. If we forget task labels, both records become the same load tuple $(2,1,2)$. Now swap the first two employees. With named employees, $(1,2,2)$ gives the single task to a different person. With unnamed employees we sort the loads and get the same tuple for both. That's the difference between partitioning the **set** $[n]$ and partitioning the **number** $n$.

For the Stirling numbers of the second kind, look at the block containing element $n$. If $n$ stands alone, partition the other $n-1$ elements into $k-1$ blocks. Otherwise partition them into $k$ blocks and choose one block for $n$ to join:

$$
S_{n,k}=S_{n-1,k-1}+kS_{n-1,k}.
$$

For number partitions, count positive integers $1\le c_1\le\cdots\le c_k$ summing to $n$. Read the smallest part. If $c_1=1$, remove a 1. We can undo this by putting a 1 at the front, so each remaining partition counts once. If $c_1>1$, subtract 1 from every part. All $k$ parts remain positive and in order, and the sum drops by $k$. Adding 1 to every part reverses that case.

$$
P_{n,k}=P_{n-1,k-1}+P_{n-k,k}.
$$

The empty partition gives $S_{0,0}=P_{0,0}=1$. A positive sum has no zero-part partition, and $k>n$ is impossible. For example, $P_{7,3}=4$ from $(1,1,5),(1,2,4),(1,3,3),(2,2,3)$. Contrast this with $S_{3,2}=3$: choose which one of three labels stands alone.

One more recurrence tracks cycles rather than blocks. Let $s_{n,k}$ count permutations of $[n]$ with exactly $k$ cycles, counting fixed points as one-element cycles. The new element $n$ either makes its own cycle or is inserted immediately after one of the $n-1$ existing elements. Thus

$$
s_{n,k}=s_{n-1,k-1}+(n-1)s_{n-1,k}.
$$

Removing $n$ recovers the original permutation and the insertion position. For instance, inserting 4 after 2 in $(1,2,3)$ changes the arrow $2\mapsto3$ to $2\mapsto4\mapsto3$. There is only one arrow to restore when we erase 4. Start the recurrence with $s_{0,0}=1$, the empty permutation with no cycles.

In a fixed block of size $c$, write its smallest label first. That uses up the choice of rotation, and arranging the other labels gives $(c-1)!$ cycles. For a given size profile $(c_1,\ldots,c_k)$, count unlabeled blocks by the division rule in §8.3, then multiply by $\prod_i(c_i-1)!$. Cyclic order survives inside the blocks. Order of the cycles themselves does not.

If empty groups are allowed among $k$ indistinguishable groups, sum over the number of occupied groups, from 1 up to $\min(n,k)$ for positive $n$. If all $k$ groups must be used, take the single term at $k$. If empty groups are allowed and their labels **matter**, a labeled assignment is simply any map or any nonnegative load tuple, depending on what distinguishes the objects.

::: practice Count the same physical allocation four ways
Distribute five tasks among three nonempty groups. Count the cases where both sets are labeled, only tasks are labeled, only groups are labeled, and neither is labeled.
::: answer
The possible sorted sizes are $(1,1,3)$ and $(1,2,2)$, so $P_{5,3}=2$. When task labels matter and group labels do not, they give $\binom5{1,1,3}/2!+\binom5{1,2,2}/2!=10+15=25=S_{5,3}$. Giving the three blocks distinct labels yields $3!\cdot25=150$. If only the group labels matter, the number of positive load triples is $\binom42=6$. In each case the formula follows from which information survives.
:::

### Double-counting identities for binomial and Stirling numbers

Many sum simplifications on exams collapse to one coefficient once both sides count the same finite set in two ways:

- **Nested subset selection:** $\binom nm\binom mk=\binom nk\binom{n-k}{m-k}$ counts pairs $A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]$ with $|A|=k$ and $|B|=m$—either pick $B$ then $A\subseteq B$, or pick $A$ first and then the $m-k$ elements of $B\setminus A$ from $[n]\setminus A$.
- **Vandermonde's identity:** $\sum_{i=0}^k\binom mi\binom{n}{k-i}=\binom{m+n}{k}$ splits a $k$-element subset of $M\uplus N$ ($|M|=m,|N|=n$) by how many elements $i$ come from $M$. Setting $m=n=k$ and rewriting one $\binom ni$ as $\binom{n}{n-i}$ yields $\sum_{i=0}^n\binom ni^2=\binom{2n}{n}$.
- **Stirling subset sum:** $\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk S_{k,r}=S_{n+1,r+1}$ counts partitions of $[n+1]$ into $r+1$ nonempty blocks by isolating the block containing $n+1$: choose the $k$ elements of $[n]$ that do **not** join $n+1$ in $\binom nk$ ways, put the remaining $n-k$ elements into $n+1$'s block, and partition the chosen $k$ elements into $r$ nonempty blocks in $S_{k,r}$ ways.
