In Chapter 4, composing a relation with itself gave us two steps through a middle element. Draw the pairs as arrows and that middle element becomes a vertex we can point to. We can now ask how far the arrows take us, which vertices can reach each other, and how two graphs move together.

## 9.1 Directed Paths, Distance, and Adjacency

Suppose $a$ sends a message to $b$, and $b$ can send it to $c$. Can $a$ reach $c$? Can $c$ reach $a$? We cannot answer either question by looking at where the vertices are drawn. Only the direction and incidence of each edge matter.

A directed graph $G=(V,E)$ uses an endorelation $E\subseteq V\times V$. Write $uE$ for the successors of $u$ and $Eu$ for its predecessors. The edge $(u,u)$ is a loop. A subgraph may remove vertices and edges. The **induced** subgraph $G[U]$ must keep every edge whose endpoints both lie in $U$, so its edge set is $E\cap(U\times U)$. For example, in the graph with edges $(a,b),(b,c),(a,c)$, the induced graph on $\{a,c\}$ contains $(a,c)$. Deleting that edge gives another subgraph on the same vertices.

The ordered sequence $(v_0,\ldots,v_\ell)$ is a path of length $\ell$ if $v_iEv_{i+1}$ at each step. Here paths may repeat vertices. A **simple path** has pairwise distinct vertices. As a relation, $E^\ell$ records existence of a path of exactly $\ell$ edges, and $E^*$ records existence without a prescribed length. The distance

$$
d(u,v)=\min\bigl(\{k\in\mathbb N_0:(u,v)\in E^k\}\cup\{\infty\}\bigr)
$$

is $\infty$ when no path exists. It can be asymmetric. In the chain $a\to b\to c$, $d(a,c)=2$ while $d(c,a)=\infty$. Also $d(a,a)=0$ without any loop. If a shortest path repeated a vertex, deleting the segment between repetitions would leave a shorter path with the same endpoints. In a finite graph on $n$ vertices, any finite distance is therefore less than $n$.

Fix a vertex enumeration $v_1,\ldots,v_n$. The adjacency matrix $M$ has $M_{ij}=1$ exactly when $(v_i,v_j)\in E$. Rows refer to departure vertices and columns to arrival vertices. We can use ordinary matrix multiplication to count paths, or Boolean matrix multiplication to detect their existence.

In $(M^2)_{ij}=\sum_t M_{it}M_{tj}$, keep the start and finish fixed and look at one term, $M_{it}M_{tj}$. The first factor asks whether we can go from $v_i$ to $v_t$. The second asks whether we can go on from $v_t$ to $v_j$. Both have to be 1 for that term to count a route. In the chain $a\to b\to c$, choosing $b$ in the middle contributes 1 to the entry from $a$ to $c$. If a second middle vertex also worked, the ordinary sum would give 2. The Boolean sum would still give 1 because it answers whether a route exists. For higher powers each summand combines a path of length $k$ with one final edge. Each sequence has exactly one penultimate vertex, so it counts once.

::: practice Distinguish a path count from a distance
Let $E=\{(a,b),(a,c),(b,d),(c,d),(a,d)\}$. What are $d(a,d)$, the $(a,d)$ entry of $M^2$, and the $(d,a)$ entry of $M^2$?
::: answer
There is a direct edge, so $d(a,d)=1$. The intermediate vertex can be $b$ or $c$, so the first matrix entry is $2$. The vertex $d$ has no outgoing edges, so the second entry is $0$. The Boolean square would give 1 at $(a,d)$.
:::

## 9.2 Cycles, DAGs, and Order

A cycle is a closed path with at least one edge. In a directed graph a loop is a cycle of length one. A cycle is simple when the vertices before the final repeated starting vertex are pairwise distinct. If a cycle repeats other vertices, choose equal occurrences at minimum positive separation. The section between them is a simple cycle. A directed graph with no cycles is a **DAG**.

Reachability $E^*$ in a DAG is a partial order. Reflexivity comes from zero steps and transitivity from concatenation. If distinct $u,v$ could each reach the other, joining the two paths would give a cycle, contradicting acyclicity. So reachability is also antisymmetric. In the other direction, the edges of a Hasse diagram always point along strict increases, so they cannot close a cycle. Given $a\to b\to c$ and an extra edge $a\to c$, try removing just the extra edge. We can still reach $c$ from $a$ through $b$. That intermediate vertex explains why the direct edge is absent from the Hasse diagram.

A finite DAG admits a topological order: an enumeration in which every edge points forward. Find a vertex with no predecessor, put it next in the list, then remove it and continue. If every vertex of a nonempty graph had a predecessor, repeatedly following predecessors would eventually repeat a vertex and produce a cycle. So the process can proceed at every stage of a DAG. Conversely, a cycle cannot keep moving strictly forward in a finite enumeration and return to its start.

::: practice Find what the drawing hides
Let $E=\{(a,c),(b,c),(c,d),(a,d)\}$. Give two topological orders and the Hasse edges of the resulting order.
::: answer
Both $(a,b,c,d)$ and $(b,a,c,d)$ work because nothing forces a relative order between $a$ and $b$. The covers are $(a,c),(b,c),(c,d)$. Reachability through $c$ accounts for $(a,d)$ even after the direct edge is removed.
:::

## 9.3 Connected Components and Quotients

Consider arrows $a\to b$, $b\to a$ and $b\to c$. Follow the last arrow to $c$, then try to get back. There is no outgoing arrow at $c$. If we allow movement in either direction along an arrow, there is a way back through $b$. The drawing stays the same. The answer depends on which moves we allow.

- Weak connectivity uses $(E\cup E^\top)^*$. Its equivalence classes are the connected components, or CCs.
- Mutual directed reachability uses $E^*\cap(E^*)^\top$. Its equivalence classes are the strongly connected components, or SCCs.

In our three-vertex example the SCCs are $\{a,b\}$ and $\{c\}$. Mutual reachability is reflexive by zero steps and symmetric by its definition. To prove transitivity, concatenate the directed paths from first to second and second to third, then concatenate in reverse order the paths returning to the first. For weak connectivity the symmetric edge relation already allows every path to be reversed. Thus both computations really give partitions, not overlapping clusters.

For any equivalence $\sim$ on $V$, collapse each class to a vertex. The quotient is

$$
G/{\sim}=\bigl(V/{\sim},\{([s]_{\sim},[t]_{\sim}):(s,t)\in E\}\bigr).
$$

An edge between classes means that **at least one** original edge has those classes as endpoints. Edges inside a class become loops. For the graph of intercomponent edges, remove those loops. Why can this graph have no cycle when the classes are SCCs? Suppose a cycle of classes enters one SCC at $x$ and takes its next cross edge out of $y$. Within that SCC there is a path from $x$ to $y$, so we can actually follow the cycle in the original graph. The same move works at every class on the cycle, giving mutual reachability across classes that were supposed to be distinct. Hence the intercomponent graph is a DAG. For an arbitrary equivalence we cannot assume the internal path from $x$ to $y$. Collapsing the successor graph on $\mathbb Z$ modulo 3, for example, gives a directed three-cycle.

## 9.4 Isomorphisms and Automorphisms

A graph isomorphism $\beta:V_G\to V_H$ is a bijection with

$$
uE_Gv\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\beta(u)E_H\beta(v).
$$

Both directions are needed. The map must preserve edges, and it must also reflect them, so the renamed graph has exactly the target edges. Applying $\beta$ to every entry of a path gives a path of the same length. Applying $\beta^{-1}$ recovers the original. Thus loops, in- and outdegrees, distances, and CC and SCC structures are preserved. A mismatch proves nonisomorphism. Agreement on degree alone does not prove isomorphism. The cycle on six vertices and the disjoint union of two triangles both have six vertices of degree two, but have different numbers of components.

**Work from the most constrained vertex.** Suppose $G$ has a loop at 1 and edges $(2,1),(3,2),(4,2)$. Suppose $H$ has a loop at $d$ and edges $(c,d),(a,c),(b,c)$. The unique loop forces $1\mapsto d$. Then the only other predecessor of $d$ forces $2\mapsto c$. The remaining leaves 3 and 4 can go to $a,b$ in either order. In each case check the four mapped edges. The map is a bijection on vertices and matches all four target edges, so it also reflects edges.

An automorphism is an isomorphism from a graph to itself. Composing isomorphisms and inverting them preserves their edge condition. Fix one isomorphism $\beta:G\to H$. Given any other $\gamma:G\to H$, go to $H$ by $\gamma$ and come back by $\beta^{-1}$. The result $\alpha=\beta^{-1}\circ\gamma$ is an automorphism of $G$. Conversely, apply any such $\alpha$ first and then $\beta$ to get an isomorphism to $H$. These operations undo each other, so enumerating the automorphisms gives every isomorphism exactly once.

For an undirected cycle on $\mathbb Z_n$ with $n\ge3$, choose the image of 0 in $n$ ways and the image of its neighbour 1 in two ways. Every next image is the neighbour distinct from the preceding image. These $2n$ choices are the rotations and reflections. For a consistently oriented cycle only the forward neighbour is legal, leaving $n$ rotations. The same vertex drawing supports different symmetries when the edge relation changes.

### Generating sets and disconnected graphs with repeated components

Listing every automorphism one by one is impractical once $|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|$ grows large, so exams often ask for $|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|$ together with a **small generating set** $\Sigma\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ whose compositions produce the whole group:

- **Path $P_n$ ($n\ge2$) on $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$:** $|\operatorname{Aut}(P_n)|=2$, generated by the single endpoint-reversing involution $(0,\,n-1)(1,\,n-2)\cdots$.
- **Cycle $C_n$ ($n\ge3$) on $\mathbb Z_n$:** $|\operatorname{Aut}(C_n)|=2n$, generated by **two** permutations—the rotation $(0\,1\,\ldots\,n-1)$ and one reflection such as $(1,\,n-1)(2,\,n-2)\cdots$.
- **Complete graph $K_n$ ($n\ge2$) on $[n]$:** $|\operatorname{Aut}(K_n)|=n!$, and the entire symmetric group $\mathcal S_n$ is generated by **at most two** permutations—one adjacent swap $(1\,2)$ and one full $n$-cycle $(1\,2\,\ldots\,n)$ (rotating $(1\,2)$ around the cycle produces every adjacent swap, and adjacent swaps sort any permutation).

For a **disjoint union** $G$ of connected components, any automorphism maps each connected component onto an isomorphic connected component. Non-isomorphic components cannot be exchanged, so their automorphism groups act independently on disjoint vertex sets. When $m$ connected components $H_1,\ldots,H_m$ are pairwise isomorphic copies of the same connected graph $H$, automorphisms can **both** apply an automorphism of $H$ inside each copy **and** permute the $m$ whole copies in $m!$ ways, contributing $|\operatorname{Aut}(H)|^m\cdot m!$ to $|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|$. Even better, to generate that whole block you only need generators of $\operatorname{Aut}(H_1)$ on the first copy together with component-swapping permutations (a swap and an $m$-cycle of the $m$ copies): conjugating a generator on $H_1$ by a component swap automatically transports that symmetry onto the other copies! For instance, on $G=P_6\oplus K_5\oplus K_5$ with disjoint vertex sets $\{1,\ldots,6\}$, $\{7,\ldots,11\}$, and $\{12,\ldots,16\}$, we have $|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|=2\cdot(5!)^2\cdot 2!=57{,}600$, generated by just **four** permutations: $(1\,6)(2\,5)(3\,4)$, $(7\,8)$, $(7\,8\,9\,10\,11)$, and the component swap $(7\,12)(8\,13)(9\,14)(10\,15)(11\,16)$.

## 9.5 Direct and Cartesian Graph Products

Two machines have states in $V_1$ and $V_2$. The combined state records a pair $(u_1,u_2)$. We have to decide whether both machines are meant to take a step together or exactly one coordinate may take a step. That decision defines two different edge relations on the same vertex set.

| Product | When $(u_1,u_2)$ can move to $(v_1,v_2)$ |
| --- | --- |
| $G_1\times G_2$ | $u_1E_1v_1$ and $u_2E_2v_2$ |
| $G_1\square G_2$ | $u_1E_1v_1$ and $u_2=v_2$, or $u_1=v_1$ and $u_2E_2v_2$ |

The **direct product** requires a genuine edge in each factor, including a loop where a coordinate stays the same. The **Cartesian product** freezes one coordinate without needing a loop at that coordinate. If both factor relations are reflexive, every Cartesian edge also appears in the direct product. The pair swap is an isomorphism reversing factor order, and regrouping triples is an isomorphism changing the parentheses of a triple product. To check either claim, compare the edge rule position by position.

Take $P_2$ with vertices 0 and 1 and its single undirected edge. The direct product $P_2\times P_2$ has just two edges, between $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ and between $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$. Each step flips **both** coordinates. The Cartesian product $P_2\square P_2$ has a square: a step flips one coordinate. There are four vertices in either case. The two-edge graph is disconnected and the square connected, so they are not isomorphic.

**Reachability in a product.** A Cartesian path projects to a path in each factor after ignoring the steps assigned to the other factor. Conversely, two factor paths can be followed one coordinate at a time. So two product vertices are in the same SCC exactly when their first coordinates lie in the same SCC of $G_1$ and their second coordinates lie in the same SCC of $G_2$. A direct path projects to two paths **of equal length**. When both factors are reflexive, any shorter path can be padded with loops until the lengths match. Equivalently, each direct step can be split into two Cartesian steps, and each Cartesian edge is already a direct edge. The products then have identical reachability.
