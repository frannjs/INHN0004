Keep only remainders modulo $6$. Doubling $2$ gives $4$, and doubling $5$ gives $10$, which also leaves $4$. Could we recover the starting number from that remainder? We’ll use divisibility to see why these two answers coincide and what changes when a multiplication can be undone.

## 12.1 Primes, Divisibility, and Factorization

### What does the bar say?

The statement $a\mid b$ means there is an integer $k$ such that $b=ka$. Equivalently, $b\in a\mathbb Z=\{ka:k\in\mathbb Z\}$. The left entry is the factor we are testing. For example, $6\mid -42$ because $-42=(-7)6$. Every integer divides $0$, including $0$ itself, and $0$ divides only $0$. The definition by a witness $k$ handles these cases without dividing by zero.

On $\mathbb N_0$, divisibility is a partial order. Here $1$ lies below every element and $0$ lies above every element. To put $6$ below $30$, find a multiplier. Here it is $5$. A greatest element of a set in this order is divisible by every member of that set.

The greatest common divisor and least common multiple are the greatest lower bound and least upper bound of $\{|a|,|b|\}$ in this order. When both inputs are nonzero, find these by taking the largest positive common divisor and the smallest positive common multiple. If one input is $0$, the divisibility order gives $\gcd(a,0)=|a|$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(a,0)=0$.

### Why the smallest divisor is useful

A prime is an integer $p\ge2$ with exactly two positive divisors, $1$ and $p$. Write $\mathbb P$ for the primes. An integer $n\ge2$ which is not prime is composite. For any $n>1$, let $d$ be its smallest divisor greater than $1$. If $d$ were composite, it would have a divisor $e$ with $1<e<d$. Since $e\mid d$ and $d\mid n$, then $e\mid n$. This contradicts the choice of $d$. So $d$ is prime.

To check primality of $n\ge2$, try primes $p\le \sqrt n$. If composite $n=ab$ with $1<a,b<n$, at least one of $a,b$ is at most $\sqrt n$, since otherwise their product is larger than $n$. A prime divisor of that smaller factor also divides $n$. For $91$ we try $2,3,5,7$ and find $91=7\cdot13$. For $97$ the same four primes fail, which proves primality. The condition $n\ge2$ matters. The number $1$ has no prime divisor and is not prime.

**Euclid's theorem.** There are infinitely many primes. Suppose $p_1,\ldots,p_m$ were all of them. Form $N=p_1\cdots p_m+1$. When divided by any listed prime, $N$ leaves remainder $1$. Yet $N>1$ has some prime divisor. That divisor lies outside the list. The argument finds a new prime divisor even if $N$ itself is composite.

### Read a number by its prime multiplicities

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says each positive integer has a unique prime factorization up to order. The empty product gives $1$. Write $\nu_p(n)=\max\{k\in\mathbb N_0:p^k\mid n\}$ for $n>0$. Thus $\nu_3(360)=2$. We set $\nu_p(0)=\infty$ when comparing multiplicities for divisibility. For positive integers, $a\mid b$ exactly when $\nu_p(a)\le\nu_p(b)$ for every prime $p$.

Existence follows by strong induction. For $n>1$, take a prime divisor $p$ and apply the hypothesis to the strictly smaller positive integer $n/p$. For uniqueness, use the prime case of Euclid's lemma proved in §12.3. A prime dividing a product must divide one of its factors. If two prime lists multiply to the same number, the first prime in one list must occur in the other. Cancel one occurrence and repeat. The remaining lists agree, including multiplicities.

Compare $360=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5$ and $126=2\cdot3^2\cdot7$. Look at the prime $2$. A divisor common to both numbers can contain at most one copy of $2$, because it has to divide $126$. A common multiple needs at least three copies, because $360$ has to divide it. Make that same comparison prime by prime. Taking every smaller exponent gives $\gcd(360,126)=2\cdot3^2=18$. Taking every larger exponent gives $\operatorname{lcm}(360,126)=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5\cdot7=2520$.

Put those two answers back together. For each prime, its smaller and larger exponents add up to the two original exponents. So for positive $a,b$, $\gcd(a,b)\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab$. When the gcd is $1$, there is no shared prime divisor and we call the inputs coprime.

## 12.2 Congruences, Residue Classes, and Units

### An integer, its class, and its remainder

Fix $n>0$. We write $a\equiv_n b$ when $n\mid(b-a)$. This is an equivalence relation because divisibility by $n$ holds for $0$, is preserved under negation, and is preserved under addition of differences. Its class is $[a]_n=a+n\mathbb Z=\{a+kn:k\in\mathbb Z\}$. For example, $[-2]_5=[3]_5$ because $3-(-2)=5$. The shared class is infinite. Its standard representative is the single integer $3$.

The classes partition $\mathbb Z$. Modulo $5$, for instance, $-2$, $3$, and $8$ sit in one block, and its label is $3$. The quotient $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ is the set of blocks, and $\mathbb Z_n=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$ is the set of their labels, the standard representatives. The unique representative of $a$ is $a\bmod n=a-n\lfloor a/n\rfloor$.

Try the division for $-7$ modulo $5$. A quotient of minus one would leave a remainder of minus two, outside the required range. Move down to minus two for the quotient and the remainder is three. That’s where the floor matters. Since $\lfloor -7/5\rfloor=-2$, we get $(-7)\bmod5=3$.

If $a'=a+kn$ and $b'=b+\ell n$, then $a'+b'-(a+b)=n(k+\ell)$ and $a'b'-ab=n(kb+\ell a+k\ell n)$. Both differences are divisible by $n$. So we can replace inputs by convenient representatives inside a sum or product. Addition $[a]_n+[b]_n=[a+b]_n$ and multiplication $[a]_n\cdot[b]_n=[ab]_n$ are well-defined, independently of how we name the classes. On representatives write $a+_n b=(a+b)\bmod n$ and $a\cdot_n b=(ab)\bmod n$.

For example, $11\equiv_{13}-2$ and $5^2\equiv_{13}-1$. Therefore
$$
5^5+8\cdot 11\equiv_{13}(-1)^2\cdot5-16\equiv_{13}2.
$$
Use congruence through the computation and convert to the standard remainder at the end. Similarly, $10\equiv_3 1$, so the remainder of a decimal integer modulo $3$ agrees with the remainder of its digit sum. For $m$ bits, another application is $-x\equiv_{2^m}(2^m-1-x)+1$. Subtracting from the all-ones word flips its bits, and the final $1$ produces the negative residue.

To require two congruences to the same number at once, require the difference to be a multiple of both moduli. The intersection of $\equiv_m$ and $\equiv_n$ is therefore $\equiv_{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}$. And $n\mid m$ exactly when $\equiv_m$ is contained in $\equiv_n$. For the reverse direction, apply the inclusion to $0\equiv_m m$.

### Which remainders have multiplicative inverses?

Compare $2x\equiv_6 4$ with $5x\equiv_6 4$. In the first, $x=2$ and $x=5$ both work, because their difference $3$ becomes $0$ modulo $6$ after multiplying by $2$. In the second, $5\cdot5\equiv_6 1$. Multiplying both sides by $5$ gives the unique solution $x\equiv_6 2$. Division requires an inverse.

For $n>1$, the units are $\mathbb Z_n^*=\{a\in\mathbb Z_n:\gcd(a,n)=1\}$. The reason is Bézout's identity from the next section. If $\gcd(a,n)=1$, then $a\alpha+n\beta=1$, so $\alpha\bmod n$ is the inverse of $a$. Conversely, $a\alpha\equiv_n1$ gives an integer combination equal to $1$, so the gcd must divide $1$.

The totient $\varphi(n)=|\mathbb Z_n^*|$ counts remainders outside the multiples of the distinct primes dividing $n$. For instance, out of $15$ remainders, remove $5$ multiples of $3$ and $3$ multiples of $5$, then restore the common multiple $0$. The result is $8$. For a set of distinct prime divisors $p_1,\ldots,p_t$, the intersection of their multiples has exactly $n/(p_1\cdots p_t)$ residues. Inclusion–exclusion gives
$$
\varphi(n)=n\prod_{p\in\mathbb P,\ p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right).
$$
Expansion produces exactly the inclusion–exclusion terms. In particular $\varphi(p^k)=p^k-p^{k-1}$, and $\varphi(mn)=\varphi(m)\varphi(n)$ for coprime $m,n$ because their sets of prime divisors are disjoint.

## 12.3 The Extended Euclidean Algorithm and Linear Congruences

### Two passes over the same table

Let $0<a\le b$. Divide $b=ka+r$ with $k=\lfloor b/a\rfloor$ and $0\le r<a$. A divisor of both $a$ and $b$ divides $r=b-ka$. Conversely a divisor of both $a$ and $r$ divides $b=ka+r$. The common divisors agree, so $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(r,a)$. Each step decreases the first entry. When $r=0$, that entry is the gcd.

Bézout's lemma gives integers $\alpha,\beta$ with $\gcd(a,b)=\alpha a+\beta b$. In the last row the gcd is the first entry, so its coefficients are $(\alpha,\beta)=(1,0)$. Now move up one row. We know how to express the gcd using $(r,a)$, and we want to express the same number using $a$ and $b$. Only the $r$ needs replacing. With $\alpha',\beta'$ from the row below, substitute $r=b-ka$.
$$
\alpha' r+\beta'a=(\beta'-k\alpha')a+\alpha'b.
$$
So $(\alpha,\beta)=(\beta'-k\alpha',\alpha')$. Look at the two contributions to the coefficient of $a$. One is already there, and the other comes from opening up the remainder. The coefficient of $b$ comes entirely from that remainder.

| $a$ | $b$ | $k$ | $\alpha$ | $\beta$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 17 | 24 | 1 | −7 | 5 |
| 7 | 17 | 2 | 5 | −2 |
| 3 | 7 | 2 | −2 | 1 |
| 1 | 3 | 3 | 1 | 0 |

Read this table in two directions when checking your work. Downwards, the next first entry must match $b-ka$ (for example, $17-2\cdot7=3$). Upwards, the new coefficients must reconstruct the gcd in their own row. The first row certifies $-7\cdot17+5\cdot24=1$, making $17^{-1}\equiv_{24}-7\equiv_{24}17$. Check the standard representative directly by multiplying $17\cdot17=289=12\cdot24+1$. For arbitrary integers take absolute values, order the pair, then restore signs and order in the coefficients. For one zero input the nonzero absolute value is already the gcd.

### Euclid's lemma tells us when we can cancel

If $d\mid ab$ and $\gcd(d,a)=1$, then $d\mid b$. Start with $\alpha a+\delta d=1$ and multiply by $b$ to write $b=\alpha ab+\delta db$. Both summands are divisible by $d$. In particular, if $p$ is prime and $p\mid ab$, then $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$. When $p$ does not divide $a$, the gcd of $p$ and $a$ is $1$. This justifies the uniqueness argument for prime factorization. It also explains why positive powers of coprime numbers stay coprime.

Bézout gives $a\mathbb Z+b\mathbb Z=\gcd(a,b)\mathbb Z$. Every integer combination is a multiple of the gcd, and the gcd itself is an integer combination. The corresponding intersection is $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)\mathbb Z$ because its elements are precisely the common multiples.

For $ax\equiv_n c$, first compute $d=\gcd(a,n)$. The equation asks whether we can write $c=ax-ny$ with integers $x,y$. Every such combination is a multiple of $d$. And if $c$ is a multiple of $d$, take a Bézout combination for $d$ and multiply it by $c/d$ to get one for $c$. This is why the divisibility test decides existence before we try to solve for $x$.

When $d\mid c$, divide the integer equation by $d$ and return to congruence notation.
$$
\frac ad\,x\equiv_{n/d}\frac cd.
$$
When $d=n$, the reduced modulus is $1$ and every $x$ works. Otherwise the reduced coefficient is a unit, so an EEA coefficient gives one residue class modulo $n/d$. Within $\mathbb Z_n$, start at that class's smallest nonnegative representative and step by $n/d$. There is one hit in each of the $d$ consecutive blocks of length $n/d$.

For example $14x\equiv_{30}8$ reduces to $7x\equiv_{15}4$. Since $7\cdot13\equiv_{15}1$, we get $x\equiv_{15}7$. Among the remainders modulo $30$, that gives $7$ from the first block and $22$ from the second.
