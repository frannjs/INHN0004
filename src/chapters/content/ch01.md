One row of a truth table can refute an argument. The work is finding a row where the premises are all true and the conclusion is false. Let's start with the simplest version of that test, a single rule that someone might break.

## 1.1 From a sentence to something we can test

Imagine a rule for submitting an assignment. *If a submission is late, then it loses one point.* Which situation breaks that rule? A late submission that loses no point. An on-time submission can lose a point for some other reason without breaking this particular rule. Keep that late submission in mind when we reach the truth table.

A **proposition** is a declarative sentence with a truth value in the situation under discussion. “The submission arrived after midnight” is a proposition once we know which submission and which midnight. “Submit before midnight” is an instruction. The question “Did it arrive?” asks for a truth value but does not itself assert one.

A **predicate** such as “submission $x$ is late” gives a proposition when we supply a particular object for $x$. We can also make a proposition by binding $x$ with a quantifier and saying which objects we are considering. Specifying that domain is part of stating the claim. The predicate $x^2=2$ has a real solution and no rational solution.

The symbol $\neg$ negates a truth value, and $\land$ asks for both values to be true. In $\neg(A\land B)$ we first answer “Are both true?” and flip that answer. In $(\neg A)\land B$ we first flip $A$ and then ask for both. Try the same input both times, with $A$ and $B$ false. The first expression becomes $\neg\text{false}$, which is true. The second has a false right operand, so it is false. One row is enough to establish that the readings differ.

A **syntax tree** draws those brackets for us. Put the outer operation at the root and make each operand a child. Negation sits at the root of the first tree. Conjunction sits at the root of the second. That root is the last operation we evaluate. Negation binds most strongly in the course convention. Use brackets to settle the order of the binary connectives.

Both trees use the same leaves and connectives. The position of the negation changes which value it receives, and the same input assignment then gives two different root values.

## 1.2 Truth tables and the conditional

A connective takes truth values as inputs. If we have two atomic propositions $A,B$, their joint truth values split all situations into four cases. We will use T for true and F for false here. “Or” is inclusive, so its output is true in the row where both inputs are true. Exclusive or, $\oplus$, means that exactly one input is true.

| $A$ | $B$ | $\neg A$ | $A\land B$ | $A\lor B$ | $A\to B$ | $A\leftrightarrow B$ | $A\oplus B$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F | F | T | F | F | T | T | F |
| F | T | T | F | T | T | F | T |
| T | F | F | F | T | F | F | T |
| T | T | F | T | T | T | T | F |

Look at the $A$ true, $B$ false row of $A\to B$. There it is again, the late submission that loses no point. That is the one failure case $A\land\neg B$.

Now look at the first two rows. In both, $A$ is false, so the rule places no restriction on $B$. In the other rows, with $A$ true, $B$ has to hold. Put those two ways of satisfying the rule in one expression and we get $\neg A\lor B$.

The converse is $B\to A$. It fails in a different row, with $B$ true and $A$ false. The contrapositive $\neg B\to\neg A$ fails in exactly the same row as $A\to B$. The biconditional $A\leftrightarrow B$ demands both conditionals, so it holds when the two truth values agree.

A propositional form is **satisfiable** if at least one assignment makes it true, **valid** if every assignment makes it true, and **unsatisfiable** if every assignment makes it false. For $A\to B$, a row with $A$ false establishes satisfiability, while $A$ true and $B$ false refutes validity. To prove that a form such as $A\lor\neg A$ is valid by cases, split on the value of $A$. In each case one disjunct holds. Its negation is unsatisfiable by the same two cases. These are claims about a whole truth column, whereas $\to$ is the connective used to calculate one entry at a time.

When the conditional holds in every situation under consideration, $A$ is **sufficient** for $B$ and $B$ is **necessary** for $A$. For the integer example, if $x/2\in\mathbb Z$, then $x\in\mathbb Z$. The reverse fails at $x=1$. Requiring $x\in 2\mathbb Z$ gives both directions, since $x/2\in\mathbb Z$ means exactly that $x=2z$ for some integer $z$. A conditional can be true at one value without being valid across the domain. The distinction between $\to$ inside a formula and a claim about validity will matter again in formal logic.

An if-then-else selects a branch. The expression *if $A$ then $B$ else $C$* has value $B$ in a row with $A$ true and value $C$ in a row with $A$ false. Its formula is $((A\to B)\land(\neg A\to C))$. To see why, fix $A$ true. The second conditional is automatically true and the first evaluates to $B$. Repeat with $A$ false and the two roles switch. Setting $C$ to true recovers the plain conditional.

## 1.3 Quantifiers and witnesses

Suppose $S(x)$ says “$x$ is a square” and $R(x)$ says “$x$ is a rectangle.” To say **every square is a rectangle**, we need to rule out a square outside the rectangles. To say **some square is a rectangle**, we need a witness inside both. These different jobs explain why the restricted universal uses a conditional and the restricted existential uses a conjunction.

| Claim | Formula | What decides it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Every $x$ with $P(x)$ has $Q(x)$ | $\forall x\,(P(x)\to Q(x))$ | One $x$ with $P(x)$ true and $Q(x)$ false refutes it |
| Some $x$ with $P(x)$ has $Q(x)$ | $\exists x\,(P(x)\land Q(x))$ | One $x$ having both properties establishes it |

The same reasoning gives the rules for negating quantifiers. “It is false that every element of $S$ has property $P$” says there is an element of $S$ failing $P$. “There is no element of $S$ having $P$” says every element of $S$ fails $P$. Now empty out $S$. The universal is true because there is no possible counterexample. That is **vacuous truth**. The existential is false because there is no possible witness. So a claim about “all” leaves open whether there is anyone to check.

Over the nonnegative integers $\mathbb N_0=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, read $\forall x\,\exists y\,(y>x)$ as two choices in that order. First we are given $x$. By the time we choose $y$, we know how large $x$ is, so $y=x+1$ works.

Read $\exists y\,\forall x\,(y>x)$ the same way. This time we have to choose $y$ first. Whatever value we choose, $x=y$ is an allowed response and makes $y>x$ false. The order tells us who gets to see whose choice.

::: practice Translate a negation
Negate “For every integer $x$ there is an integer $y$ such that $x+y=0$.” Is the negation true?
::: answer
The negation says that there is an integer $x$ such that every integer $y$ satisfies $x+y\ne0$. It is false. For any proposed $x$, the choice $y=-x$ is an integer and makes the sum zero. Flip both quantifiers and negate the predicate at the end.
:::

## 1.4 Validity and inference

An argument has premises $P_1,\ldots,P_n$ and a conclusion $C$. It is **valid** when no situation makes all premises true and the conclusion false. The whole claim is the validity of $((P_1\land\cdots\land P_n)\to C)$. A truth table checks that condition by exhaustion. A direct argument can instead concentrate on what happens once the premises are true.

For example, assume $A\to B$ and assume $A$. We are now on a row where $A$ is true. The conditional being true rules out the row with $B$ false, so $B$ holds. This is **modus ponens**. If $A\to B$ and $\neg B$ hold, the same failure row now rules out $A$, giving **modus tollens**.

| Premises | Conclusion | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $A\lor B$, $\neg B$ | $A$ | At least one disjunct holds and $B$ fails |
| $A\to B$, $B\to C$ | $A\to C$ | If $A$ holds, follow the two conditionals in order |
| $A\lor B$, $A\to C$, $B\to C$ | $C$ | Each possible true disjunct gives $C$ |
| $A\to B$, $B$ | No guarantee of $A$ | $A$ false, $B$ true is consistent with both premises |
| $A\to B$, $\neg A$ | No guarantee of $\neg B$ | The same row satisfies both premises and has $B$ true |

“Valid” tells us what follows once the premises hold. For instance, modus ponens still has the same valid form if $A$ stands for “$2$ is odd.” To use it to establish a fact about $B$, we also have to establish $A$. When the conclusion is quantified, the job changes with its first word. For “every,” take an arbitrary object. For “some,” find an object that can serve as the witness.

::: manim ch01_euler_inference.mp4 | Where the witness must lie
Follow one point through the nested regions. In the second example, watch for a point that meets both premises while falling outside the claimed conclusion.
:::

For example, suppose all $P$ objects are $Q$ and all $Q$ objects are $R$. Pick an arbitrary $d$ with $P(d)$. The first premise gives $Q(d)$ and the second gives $R(d)$. Because we used no special property of $d$ besides $P(d)$, every $P$ object is $R$. In a different argument, “some $P$ is $Q$” supplies a particular $d$ having both. If all $P$ objects are $R$, that same $d$ witnesses “some $R$ is $Q$.” An Euler diagram encodes the required inclusion and location of that witness. For a counterexample, draw or describe one configuration consistent with all premises that puts the witness in a forbidden region.

::: practice Two subsets of the same region
“All $P$ are $R$” and “all $Q$ are $R$” are true. Must some $P$ be $Q$, even when $P,Q$ are both nonempty?
::: answer
No. Take a domain with two objects $p$ and $q$. Let $R$ hold of both, $P$ only of $p$ and $Q$ only of $q$. Both smaller regions lie in $R$, but they can be disjoint.
:::

## 1.5 Choosing a proof

For a direct proof of $A\to B$, assume $A$ and look for steps that give $B$. For a proof by **contraposition**, assume $\neg B$ and establish $\neg A$. These openings work on the same conditional and may expose very different useful facts.

To show that an integer $z$ with $z^2$ even must be even, try the contrapositive. Starting with an odd $z$ gives us something we can substitute, $z=2k+1$. Squaring gives $z^2=2(2k^2+2k)+1$. Look at the $+1$ outside the even part. It shows that the square is odd, which is exactly what the contrapositive needed.

In a proof by **contradiction**, assume the negation of the target and show that this assumption, together with established facts, leads to incompatible statements. To show $\sqrt2$ is irrational, assume it equals $m/n$ in lowest terms with $m,n$ positive integers. Then $m^2=2n^2$. The previous argument gives that $m$ is even, so write $m=2k$. Substitution gives $n^2=2k^2$ and therefore $n$ is even as well. Both can be divided by $2$, contrary to the choice of lowest terms. The contradiction concerns the representation of the assumed rational number.

A **definition** fixes the meaning of a term. A **lemma** is a proved result used on the way to another result. A **theorem** states a principal result, and a **corollary** follows from an earlier result. When reading a proof, identify its hypotheses, the conclusion, and exactly where each hypothesis is used. When writing one, check that every step follows under the stated domain and assumptions.

::: practice Prove a conditional the shorter way
For integers $a,b$, prove that if $ab$ is odd then both $a$ and $b$ are odd.
::: answer
Try the contrapositive because “one factor is even” yields a factor of $2$ in the product immediately. If $a=2k$ then $ab=2(kb)$ is even. If $b=2k$ then $ab=2(ak)$ is even. In either case the product is even. This proves the original claim by contraposition.
:::
