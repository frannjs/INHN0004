The degree list told us something about a graph without telling us which edges it had. Keep those counting tools nearby. We'll see whether a drawing can avoid crossings, use edges to record conflicts, and work out when a tour can cover a graph.

## 11.1 Planarity and Euler’s Formula

Draw $K_4$ as a square with both diagonals. They cross. Does that settle whether $K_4$ is planar? Move one vertex inside a triangle formed by the other three and join it to the triangle's vertices. Now nothing crosses. Planarity is a property of whether **some** drawing works.

A graph is planar if it admits a drawing in the plane with distinct vertices and edges meeting only at common endpoints. One such drawing is a planar embedding. Wagner–Fáry's theorem says that requiring line segments in place of curves does not change which simple graphs are planar. The drawing divides the plane into faces, including the unbounded face. To see why a face boundary may repeat an edge, attach a leaf just outside the triangle in the $K_4$ drawing. Go around the outside. You'll walk out to the leaf and back on the same edge, and the number of faces stays the same.

For a finite planar graph with $k$ connected components,

$$
|V|-|E|+|F|=1+k.
$$

Start with a spanning forest inside the embedding. It has $|V|-k$ edges and just one face, since trees enclose no region. Put back the remaining edges one by one. Each connects two vertices already in the same component. It lies in a face and splits that face into two: there is already a simple path between its endpoints, and that path with the new edge encloses a region. Both $|E|$ and $|F|$ increase by one. The equation therefore remains true. For a connected drawing the right-hand side is 2. A square and its diagonal give $4-5+3=2$: the two bounded triangles and the outside make three faces.

**Use the edges twice.** In a connected embedding the sum of face boundary lengths counts each edge side once, giving $2|E|$. If every face has length at least $d>2$, then $d|F|\le 2|E|$. Combine with Euler's equation and rearrange:

$$
|E|\le \frac{d}{d-2}(|V|-2).
$$

For a connected simple graph on at least three vertices, a face boundary cannot have length 1, since that needs a loop. A boundary of length 2 would have to go out and back on a single edge, with no other edge to follow at either end. That would be the connected graph on just two vertices. So every boundary has length at least 3, giving $|E|\le3|V|-6$. The bound also holds for disconnected simple planar graphs with $|V|\ge3$, since one can add connecting edges in the outer face after arranging the components disjointly. For a connected bipartite graph with at least three vertices, the boundary walk switches parts on every edge. Its length must be even, so it is at least 4. The corresponding bound is $|E|\le2|V|-4$.

Now $K_5$ has five vertices and ten edges, exceeding $3\cdot5-6=9$. $K_{3,3}$ has six vertices and nine edges. The usual bound gives 12 and makes no decision. Bipartiteness sharpens it to 8, proving nonplanarity. A failed inequality is a certificate of nonplanarity. A satisfied inequality leaves the question open: add an isolated vertex to $K_5$, and the edge count now satisfies the bound while the subgraph $K_5$ remains nonplanar.

**Two ways to find an obstruction.** A subdivision inserts degree-two vertices along edges. Such insertions preserve planarity, since a point can be marked on an existing curve, or traversed without a vertex to reverse the operation. Kuratowski’s theorem states that a simple graph is planar exactly when it contains no subdivision of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ as a subgraph. In Wagner’s theorem the obstructions are minors. Obtain a minor by deleting edges, deleting isolated vertices, and contracting edges, merging their endpoints and retaining a simple graph. The same two graphs are forbidden minors. Think carefully about which operation the argument uses: deleting a middle vertex of a subdivided edge would break the connection, while reversing a subdivision joins its neighbours.

The face count also constrains regular planar graphs. In a connected planar graph with all degrees $p\ge3$, $p|V|=2|E|\le6|V|-12$, so $p<6$. If all face boundaries have the same length $q\ge3$, then $q|F|=2|E|$. Substituting these two relations into Euler's formula gives

$$
|V|\left(1-\frac p2+\frac pq\right)=2,
\qquad \frac1p+\frac1q>\frac12.
$$

The only possible pairs are $(3,3),(3,4),(3,5),(4,3),(5,3)$. For a convex regular polyhedron these are the five Platonic cases.

For a disconnected graph with at least two components, we can draw them with disjoint outer regions and add connecting bridges between components, swapping a component's two colour classes first if needed. ::: practice Count two kinds of faces
A connected simple planar graph is 3-regular. In one embedding every face boundary is a simple cycle of length 5 or 6. How many pentagonal faces are there?
::: answer
Let $f_5$ and $f_6$ count the two face types, so $|F|=f_5+f_6$. Counting face sides gives $5f_5+6f_6=2|E|$. Counting degrees gives $3|V|=2|E|$. Substituting $|V|=2|E|/3$ into Euler’s equation gives $|F|=2+|E|/3$. Multiply by 6 to get $6|F|=12+2|E|$. The difference $6|F|-2|E|$ counts one missing side for each pentagon and zero for each hexagon. Thus $f_5=12$.
:::

### Counting geometric regions with Euler's formula

When curves or straight chords divide a disk into regions (such as slicing a round pizza), turn the drawing into a connected planar graph before counting anything: place a vertex at every boundary mark and at every interior intersection point, and treat each curve segment between consecutive vertices as an edge. When no three interior chords concur, each interior crossing has degree $4$, and every boundary vertex's degree equals the number of rim arcs plus interior chords incident to it. Compute $|E|$ by the handshaking lemma $\sum_v\deg(v)=2|E|$, apply $|F|=2-|V|+|E|$, and subtract $1$ at the end to exclude the unbounded region outside the disk.

## 11.2 Vertex Colourings and Conflict Scheduling

Suppose one student takes lectures $A,B$, another $B,C$, and a third $C,D$. Join two lectures if a student takes both. The edge $\{A,B\}$ says the first student needs two different slots. Assigning time slots is now a colouring $c:V\to\mathbb N$ with $c(u)\ne c(v)$ for every edge $\{u,v\}$. In the example, $A,C$ may share one slot and $B,D$ another. The edge $\{A,B\}$ forces at least two slots, so this assignment is optimal.

The **chromatic number** $\chi(G)$ is the minimum number of colours used. An explicit colouring proves an upper bound. A subgraph that needs many colours proves a lower bound, since a colouring of $G$ restricts to it. A $K_k$ subgraph forces at least $k$ colours. A connected triangle-free graph can still need three: alternating two colours around an odd cycle fails at the closing edge, while a third colour at the last vertex works.

A simple graph admits a colouring with at most two colours exactly when it is bipartite, equivalently when it has no odd cycle. A two-colour assignment splits the vertices into two sets, and any cycle must alternate colours and hence have even length. For the converse, root a spanning tree in each component and colour by parity of depth. Along a tree path, each move flips the colour. If a graph edge joined two vertices of the same colour, the tree path between them would have even length. The edge itself would close that path into an odd cycle. Thus, in a graph with no odd cycle, the tree colouring already works for every edge.

For the mesh $M_{m,n}$ use parity of $i+j$. For $Q_d$ use parity of the number of 1 bits. In both cases a move changes parity. If at least one edge exists, two is also a lower bound. The one-vertex cases have chromatic number 1.

A greedy colouring in any vertex order uses at most $1+\max_v\deg(v)$ colours, since at most $\deg(v)$ colours are forbidden by earlier neighbours. It need not be optimal. In a minimum colouring every pair of used colours appears on the endpoints of some edge. Otherwise the two colour classes could be merged. Thus $|E|\ge\binom{\chi(G)}2$. Treat these inequalities as bounds and look for a matching lower and upper bound when the question asks for an exact number.

**Planar graphs.** The four-colour theorem gives $\chi(G)\le4$ for simple planar graphs. The five-colour proof shows why the embedding can help. Graphs with at most two vertices can be coloured directly. For a larger graph, the planar edge bound gives $2|E|\le 6|V|-12$, so the average degree is less than six. Take a vertex $u$ of degree at most five. Remove it and use induction to colour the smaller planar graph with five colours. To put $u$ back, look for a colour missing from its neighbours. The only time we have to change anything is when all five colours occur there. Name the neighbours $v_1,\ldots,v_5$ in cyclic order around $u$, and name their colours $1,\ldots,5$ accordingly. If $v_1$ and $v_3$ are in different components of the subgraph induced by colours 1 and 3, swap those two colours in the component of $v_1$.

Check the edges before putting $u$ back. An edge inside the component still joins different colours because both ends change. An edge leaving the component reaches a vertex of colour 2, 4 or 5. A vertex of colour 1 or 3 joined by that edge would be in the component. So the swap keeps the colouring valid. Now $v_1$ has colour 3, and colour 1 is free for $u$.

Otherwise a simple alternating path joins $v_1,v_3$. That path together with their two edges to $u$ forms a closed curve. The incident edges to $v_2$ and $v_4$ lie on opposite sides in the embedding. An alternating path using colours 2 and 4 from $v_2$ to $v_4$ would have to cross the curve. It cannot intersect its vertices because the two colour pairs are disjoint, and it cannot cross any drawn edge. So the component swap works for colours 2 and 4 instead, freeing colour 2. The argument uses both the cyclic order of the five neighbours and the separation of the two colour pairs.

::: practice A lower and an upper bound meet
Three activities $A,B,C$ conflict pairwise. A fourth $D$ conflicts with $B$ and $C$ only. How many slots are necessary and sufficient?
::: answer
The first three form a triangle, so at least three slots are necessary. Give them different slots and put $D$ in the slot of $A$. That is sufficient. One bound alone would only give a range for the answer.
:::

### Counting proper $k$-colourings

Keep two questions distinct: $\chi(G)$ asks for the **smallest** palette size, whereas counting **proper $k$-colourings** asks how many maps $c\colon V\to[k]$ satisfy $c(u)\ne c(v)$ on every edge $\{u,v\}$. On $K_n$, all $n$ vertices need distinct colours, giving the falling factorial $k(k-1)\cdots(k-n+1)$ (zero if $k<n$). On **any tree** $T$ with $n\ge1$ vertices—including $P_n$—root the tree at one vertex and visit the remaining vertices in parent-before-child order: the root has $k$ choices, and each subsequent vertex has only its already-coloured parent forbidding one colour, leaving $k-1$ independent choices and $k(k-1)^{n-1}$ proper $k$-colourings in total (for example, $P_{10}$ has $5\cdot 4^9$ proper $5$-colourings). Across disconnected components $G_1\oplus G_2$, colour choices are independent and multiply.

## 11.3 Eulerian Cycles, Hamiltonian Cycles, and Gray Codes

Two requests can sound almost identical. “Cross every bridge once and return” concerns edges. “Visit every town once and return” concerns vertices. An **Eulerian** cycle uses every edge exactly once and visits every vertex. A **Hamiltonian** cycle visits every vertex exactly once before returning to the start. For simple graphs, the single-vertex graph is allowed the empty cycle in these two definitions.

An Eulerian cycle exists in a finite simple graph exactly when the graph is connected and all degrees are even. For necessity, the cycle links all vertices. Every visit to a vertex pairs one incoming traversal with one outgoing traversal, so the incident edges occur in pairs.

For sufficiency, start at any vertex and follow unused edges until stuck. At a vertex other than the start, every earlier arrival was paired with a departure. The current arrival adds one more used edge. The used count there is odd, so it cannot equal the even degree. Thus the trail can only stop at its start. Remove its edges. The remaining degrees are still even since the closed trail used pairs of incidences.

If edges remain, connectedness gives us an unused edge touching the current tour. If all remaining edges were outside the tour, a path from a tour vertex to one of them would have a first edge leaving the tour's vertex set. That edge would be unused. Start a fresh closed trail there. At one occurrence of that vertex on the original tour, take the new trail back to the same vertex and then carry on. Each splice uses fresh edges and keeps the tour closed, so finiteness guarantees completion. Two disjoint triangles show where the argument needs connectedness. Each has a closed trail, and there is no shared vertex at which to splice them.

::: manim ch11_euler_splice.mp4 | Splice two closed trails into one
The arrows show the traversal direction on the undirected edges. Pause when the first trail returns to $s$. We can insert the second trail at that occurrence of $s$ and still traverse every edge once.
:::

For a finite directed graph, the corresponding test is strong connectivity and equal indegree and outdegree at every vertex, $|Ev|=|vE|$. A closed tour gives a directed route between any two visited vertices and pairs arrivals with departures. Conversely, the same trail-splicing method works with arrows. If a trail arrives at a vertex other than its start, it has used one more incoming edge than outgoing edge there, so another outgoing edge remains. After a closed trail is formed, strong connectivity gives a way from its vertices to any unused edge, ensuring some unused edge leaves a vertex on the trail. Splice in a new closed trail there.

A Hamiltonian cycle needs two neighbours at every vertex when there are at least three vertices, but even degrees are irrelevant to that requirement. A square with one diagonal has a Hamiltonian cycle around its boundary, and two odd degrees preclude an Eulerian cycle. Two triangles sharing just one vertex have an Eulerian cycle formed by traversing one triangle then the other. A Hamiltonian tour cannot visit both outer parts and return without passing through the shared vertex twice before finishing.

**Dirac’s sufficient criterion.** In a simple graph, if $n=|V|\ge3$ and every vertex has degree at least $n/2$, there is a Hamiltonian cycle. Each component must have room for a vertex and its at least $n/2$ neighbours, so two components would require more than $n$ vertices. Take a longest simple path $v_0,\ldots,v_m$. If either endpoint had a neighbour off the path, we could extend it. So both endpoint neighbourhoods lie on the path.

Imagine cutting the path between $v_i$ and $v_{i+1}$. We could close up the two pieces by adding the edges from $v_i$ to $v_m$ and from $v_{i+1}$ back to $v_0$. So let's look for a cut where both of those edges are already in the graph.

On the $m$ positions $i=0,\ldots,m-1$, mark the positions where $v_m$ is adjacent to $v_i$ and the positions where $v_0$ is adjacent to $v_{i+1}$. The sizes of the two marked sets add to at least $n>m$, so they overlap. At such a position, follow the path through $v_i$, cross to $v_m$, follow the path backward through $v_{i+1}$, and return to $v_0$. That cycle visits all the path vertices. If it omitted a vertex, connectedness would give an edge from the cycle to the outside. Breaking the cycle there and using the edge would give a longer simple path, a contradiction.

Ore’s condition keeps $n\ge3$ and asks for $\deg(v)+\deg(w)\ge n$ for every nonadjacent distinct pair. In different components, the two vertices and all their neighbours would need at least $n+2$ places. So the graph is connected. On a longest path, adjacent endpoints close the path directly. For nonadjacent endpoints, the bound gives the same overlap of marked positions, and the rest of the proof follows as before. For an idea of what the criteria leave open, try the graph $C_5$. It has a Hamiltonian cycle right there, although every degree is 2 and every pair of degrees sums to 4.

**Gray code on $Q_d$.** Start in dimension one with the list $0,1$. Prefix 0 to the old list, then prefix 1 to the *reversed* old list. Consecutive entries within a block differ in one suffix bit by induction. At the junction the suffix is identical and only the prefix changes. The first and last entries also have identical suffixes. Thus for $d\ge2$, the list gives a Hamiltonian cycle in $Q_d$. In dimension one it gives a Hamiltonian path only, since it would need to reuse its single edge to return. For $Q_3$, check the junction explicitly:

$$
000,\ 001,\ 011,\ 010,\ 110,\ 111,\ 101,\ 100.
$$

Read the sequence as a proof. The first three changes happen in the suffix. The middle change flips the leading bit, and the second half walks the suffixes in reverse. The last step back to $000$ changes just the leading bit.

::: practice Keep the two traversal tests separate
For $Q_3$, decide whether an Eulerian cycle exists and exhibit a Hamiltonian one.
::: answer
Every vertex has degree three, so there is no Eulerian cycle. The Gray-code order above visits all eight vertices once and closes by a single bit change from $100$ to $000$. Hence it is a Hamiltonian cycle.
:::
