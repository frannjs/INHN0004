Take two quarter turns of a square. Where does a marked corner end up? Then keep turning until each corner is back where it started. Combining two moves and finding the first return are different jobs. We’ll do both with rotations, remainders, and permutations.

## 13.1 Group Basics, Permutations, and Operation Tables

### The operations, including undo

Keep the rotations through multiples of $90^\circ$. Two quarter turns give a half turn, which is still in our set. If there are three turns, grouping the first two or the last two leaves the total angle unchanged. A turn by zero leaves everything in place, and a turn by $270^\circ$ undoes one by $90^\circ$. Closure, associativity, a neutral element and an inverse for every move give us the four group conditions.

A **group** $(G,\cdot,1)$ has an operation $G\times G\to G$, which is associative, a two-sided neutral element $1$, and for every $a\in G$ a two-sided inverse $a^{-1}$. When also $ab=ba$ for every pair the group is **abelian** or commutative. We may then use additive notation $(G,+,0)$ and write $-a$ for the inverse. The symbols denote the declared operation and identity. The neutral element named $1$ in multiplicative notation need not be the integer $1$. The **group order** $|G|$ is the cardinality of the carrier set.

Why are the neutral element and the inverses unique? Two neutral elements $e,f$ satisfy $ef=e$ and $ef=f$. If $b$ and $c$ are inverses of $a$, then $b=b(ac)=(ba)c=c$. The middle equality is associativity. We regroup the three factors so that the other inverse pair comes together.

That regrouping also tells us where to put an inverse when canceling. From $ax=ay$ we multiply by $a^{-1}$ on the left. From $xa=ya$ we multiply on the right. To solve $axb=c$, undo the left factor on the left and the right factor on the right to get $x=a^{-1}cb^{-1}$. To undo the whole product $ab$, the inverse factors go in reverse order. In $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1})$, the $b$ factors meet first. In $(b^{-1}a^{-1})(ab)$, the $a$ factors meet first. Both products are $1$, so $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$.

The additive group $(\mathbb Z_n,+_n,0)$ contains all remainders. The multiplicative group $(\mathbb Z_n^*,\cdot_n,1)$ contains only the units. Closure of the product follows from the prime divisor test. If a prime dividing $n$ divided the product of two units, it would divide one of them. Inverses come from the EEA. When $p$ is prime, every nonzero remainder is a unit. For composite modulus this fails. For example, $2\cdot_6 3=0$ even though neither factor is zero. The choice of operation matters even for the same set: $\mathbb Z$ is a group under addition but its nonzero elements are not a group under ordinary multiplication because $2$ has no integer multiplicative inverse.

The circle group uses rotation angles modulo a full revolution. A rotation through $2\pi k/n$ behaves like $k\in\mathbb Z_n$ under addition: composing two rotations adds their angles, with the full revolution identified with zero. In a direct product $G\times H$ we perform the two operations componentwise and take inverses in the same way, so the neutral element is $(1_G,1_H)$. This construction will separate one modular computation into two smaller ones in §13.4.

### Permutation products are read right to left

The symmetric group $\mathcal S_A$ consists of bijections $A\to A$ under function composition. If $|A|=n$, the $n$ choices for the first image leave $n-1$ for the second, yielding $n!$ permutations. In the usual case $A=[n]$, write $\mathcal S_n$. A permutation can be written as an image list, as two rows of inputs and outputs, or in cycle notation. For $(1\,2\,3)$ the images of $1,2,3$ are $2,3,1$. The identity may be denoted $\operatorname{Id}_{[n]}$.

Set $a=(1\,2)$ and $b=(2\,3)$ in $\mathcal S_3$. Start with $1$ in $a\circ b$. The right-hand permutation $b$ leaves it in place. Then $a$ sends it to $2$. Follow $2$ next. It goes to $3$ under $b$ and stays there under $a$. Finally, $b$ sends $3$ to $2$, and $a$ brings us back to $1$. So $a\circ b=(1\,2\,3)$.

Now switch the order and follow just $1$ first. The swap $a$ moves it to $2$, and $b$ takes it on to $3$. That already tells us the two products differ. Continuing around the cycle gives $b\circ a=(1\,3\,2)$. In an operation table, follow the column permutation first, then the row permutation. Recall that the relational product $RS$ in Chapter 4 follows $R$ first.

A graph automorphism is a permutation of the node set that preserves adjacency in both directions. The identity preserves adjacency. So do successive automorphisms and their inverses. Thus the automorphisms form a group under $\circ$. To enumerate them, first restrict each node's possible images by degree, then use its neighbors to rule out the remaining candidates. Preserving degree is only a necessary condition.

### Read a table as a set of bijections

For $a\in G$, the map $x\mapsto ax$ has inverse $x\mapsto a^{-1}x$, so each row of a finite operation table contains every element exactly once. Columns use $x\mapsto xa$ and have the same property. This condition alone does not prove associativity. Symmetry across the main diagonal gives commutativity because the reflected entries are $ab$ and $ba$. To read an inverse, look for the identity in the row of the element.

There is a useful structural observation here. With $\ell_a(x)=ax$, we have $\ell_a\circ\ell_b=\ell_{ab}$ and $\ell_a(1)=a$. The group therefore acts by permutations of its own elements, and distinct elements give distinct permutations. This is why an operation table can encode permutations even before we draw a graph.

::: practice A swap in a graph group
The graph has edges $\{1,2\},\{2,3\}$ and no others. Find its automorphisms. Write its operation table and explain why there are no more.
::: answer
Node $2$ has degree $2$, so it is fixed. Nodes $1$ and $3$ can be fixed or exchanged, and either choice preserves the edge set. With $e=\operatorname{Id}_{[3]}$ and $s=(1\,3)$, we obtain the following table.

| $\circ$ | $e$ | $s$ |
|---|---|---|
| $e$ | $e$ | $s$ |
| $s$ | $s$ | $e$ |

The bottom-right entry is $e$ because performing the swap twice returns every vertex to its start. The two off-diagonal entries agree, so the group is commutative.
:::

## 13.2 Modular Linear Systems, Element Orders, and Cyclic Groups

### A pivot needs an inverse

A ring has an additive abelian group and an associative multiplication which distributes over addition. In the rings here there is a multiplicative identity. In a field, the nonzero elements also form an abelian group under multiplication. Thus $\mathbb Z_p$ with its modular operations is a field for prime $p$, while $\mathbb Z_6$ is a ring with nonzero elements that have no multiplicative inverse. A monoid has an associative operation and a neutral element, with no general inverse requirement.

The goal of Gaussian elimination is to remove an unknown from an equation while keeping the same solutions. Swapping equations is reversible. So is adding a multiple of one equation to another, because we can subtract that same multiple afterward. To scale an equation and then undo the scaling, we need a unit. In $\mathbb Z_p$ with $p$ prime, every nonzero coefficient is a unit. Modulo $6$, multiplying $x\equiv_6 2$ by $2$ gives $2x\equiv_6 4$, which also admits $x=5$.

Let's solve this system over $\mathbb Z_7$.
$$
2x+3y\equiv_7 1,\qquad 4x+y\equiv_7 2.
$$
Look first at the coefficients of $x$. The second is twice the first, so subtract twice the first equation from the second. This gives $-5y\equiv_7 0$, so $2y\equiv_7 0$. At this step we do need an inverse. Since $2\cdot4\equiv_7 1$, $y=0$ and then $x=4$. Substitution gives $8\equiv_7 1$ and $16\equiv_7 2$. Each equation operation was reversible, so this is the unique solution in standard representatives.

### The order is the first return time

For $a\in G$ write $a^0=1$, $a^{k+1}=a^k a$ for $k\ge0$, and $a^{-k}=(a^{-1})^k$ for $k>0$. The cyclic subgroup $\langle a\rangle=\{a^k:k\in\mathbb Z\}$ is closed under multiplication and inverses, and $a^j a^k=a^{j+k}$. In additive notation the same construction takes all integer multiples $ka$, starting from $0$. The size of this subgroup is $\operatorname{ord}(a)$, the **order of the element**. It counts what this one element reaches and can differ from the group order $|G|$.

In $\mathbb Z_{15}^*$, repeated multiplication by $2$ takes $1\to2\to4\to8\to1$. The first return occurs after four steps. Why does that tell us the size? If $a^i=a^j$ for $0\le i<j$, then $a^{j-i}=1$. So before the first return there can be no repeat. After a return at $m$, divide any integer exponent $k$ by $m$ to leave a residue in $\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$. Thus for finite order $m$,
$$
\operatorname{ord}(a)=\min\{k>0:a^k=1\},\qquad
a^k=1\ \Longleftrightarrow\ m\mid k,\qquad
\operatorname{ord}(a^d)=\frac{m}{\gcd(m,d)}.
$$
For the last identity, each step by $a^d$ takes $d$ steps in the original cycle. We need the first positive $k$ with $m\mid dk$. Divide $m$ and $d$ by their gcd. The reduced factors are coprime, so the reduced $m$ must divide $k$ by Euclid's lemma. The first such $k$ is therefore $m/\gcd(m,d)$. If there is no positive return for $a$, all integer powers of $a$ are distinct and its order is infinite.

For a permutation, all disjoint cycles must return at once. Thus $\operatorname{ord}((1\,2\,3)(4\,5))=\operatorname{lcm}(3,2)=6$. The cycle lengths can also be used separately to compute a power: $787\equiv_3 1$ and $787\equiv_2 1$, so this permutation to the power $787$ is the original one.

A group is **cyclic** if $G=\langle a\rangle$ for some $a$, called a generator. If $g$ generates a group of order $m$, then $g^k$ generates exactly when $\gcd(k,m)=1$. There are $\varphi(m)$ generators for $m>1$. In additive $\mathbb Z_{12}$, $5$ generates because its twelve successive multiples are all distinct, whereas $4$ has order $12/\gcd(12,4)=3$.

::: practice One exponent, two cycles
Given $\pi=(1\,2\,3\,4)(5\,6\,7)$, compute $\operatorname{ord}(\pi)$ and $\operatorname{ord}(\pi^8)$.
::: answer
The first cycle returns at multiples of $4$, and the second at multiples of $3$. The first joint return is at $12$. For $\pi^8$, four points are fixed because $8$ is a multiple of $4$. The three-cycle advances by $8\bmod3=2$ at each step. That part returns after $3$ steps, agreeing with $12/\gcd(12,8)=3$.
:::

### Which multiplicative unit groups are cyclic?

Every group of **prime order** $|G|=p\in\mathbb P$ is cyclic and every nonidentity element generates it, since $\operatorname{ord}(a)\mid p$ forces $\operatorname{ord}(a)=p$ for $a\ne 1$. For multiplicative unit groups $(\mathbb Z_n^*,\cdot_n,1)$ ($n\ge2$), **Gauss's primitive-root theorem** states (without proof in the lecture) that $\mathbb Z_n^*$ is cyclic if and only if
$$
n\in\{2,\ 4,\ p^r,\ 2p^r\mid p\in\mathbb P\setminus\{2\},\ r\in\mathbb N\}.
$$
Generators of a cyclic $\mathbb Z_n^*$ are called **primitive roots modulo $n$**, and there are $\varphi(|\mathbb Z_n^*|)=\varphi(\varphi(n))$ of them. In particular:

- **Prime modulus:** For every prime $p$, $\mathbb Z_p^*$ is cyclic of order $p-1$, so $(\mathbb Z_p^*,\cdot_p,1)\cong(\mathbb Z_{p-1},+_{p-1},0)$.
- **Twice an odd prime power:** Since $\mathbb Z_2^*=\{1\}$, $\mathbb Z_{2p^r}^*\cong\mathbb Z_2^*\times\mathbb Z_{p^r}^*\cong\mathbb Z_{p^r}^*\cong\mathbb Z_{\varphi(p^r)}$ is also cyclic! For example, $3623$ is prime, so $\mathbb Z_{3623}^*\cong\mathbb Z_{7246}^*\cong\mathbb Z_{3622}$ all have order $3622$ and are cyclic.
- **When cyclicity fails:** For $2^k$ with $k\ge3$ (where $\mathbb Z_{2^k}^*\cong\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_{2^{k-2}}$, generated by $-1$ and $5$) or any product involving two distinct odd primes $pq$ (where $p-1$ and $q-1$ are both even, so $\lambda(pq)=\operatorname{lcm}(p-1,q-1)<\varphi(pq)$), no single generator exists. Likewise, $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m,0)\times(\mathbb Z_n,+_n,0)$ is cyclic iff $\gcd(m,n)=1$.

## 13.3 Subgroups, Cayley Graphs, and Euler–Fermat Theorems

### Read the partition from repeated right multiplication

A subgroup $H\le G$ uses the operation and identity of $G$. To show it is one, check that it is nonempty and $xy^{-1}\in H$ whenever $x,y\in H$. Taking $x=y$ gives the identity, then $x=1$ gives inverses, and finally using the inverse of the second element gives closure under products. Associativity is inherited.

For $x\in G$, the **left coset** $xH=\{xh:h\in H\}$ is a translated copy of $H$. Multiplication by $x$ gives a bijection $H\to xH$. If two cosets have a common element, say $xh_1=yh_2$, then $y^{-1}x=h_2h_1^{-1}\in H$. Multiplying by another member of $H$ stays in $H$, so $xH\subseteq yH$, and symmetry gives equality. All cosets therefore partition $G$. The finite version, **Lagrange's theorem**, gives $|H|\mid |G|$. In particular $\operatorname{ord}(a)\mid |G|$.

The Cayley graph $\Gamma(G,\Sigma)$ has vertices $x\in G$ and an arrow of label $a$ from $x$ to $xa$ for each $a\in\Sigma$. Use right multiplication in this picture. Following labels $a,b$ from $x$ ends at $(xa)b=x(ab)$. With one label $a$, components are exactly the cosets $x\langle a\rangle$. For several labels, they are cosets of the subgroup $\langle\Sigma\rangle$ consisting of products of generators and inverses. Traversing an arrow backwards applies the inverse. For finite $G$, even inverses can be replaced by forward steps because $a^{-1}=a^{\operatorname{ord}(a)-1}$. Thus connectedness and strong connectedness agree here, and connectedness is equivalent to generating $G$.

In $\mathbb Z_{21}^*$, multiplication by $10$ makes the two cycles $1,10,16,13,4,19$ and $8,17,2,20,11,5$. The first is $\langle10\rangle$. You get the second in the same order by multiplying every entry of the first by $8$. This is the coset $8\langle10\rangle$ drawn as a cycle. With just the label $10$ we stay in whichever cycle we started in. Add the label $8$ and the arrow $1\to8$ lets us enter the other cycle, so the identity can now reach every vertex.

Suppose we want to read $8\cdot_{21}16$ from the graph. From $1$, two steps labeled $10$ reach $16$. Take those same two steps from $8$ and arrive at $2$. The labels tell us how to apply the second factor at the starting vertex given by the first.

Track one multiplication label through a complete cycle. Next watch a second label connect the components. In the final part, compare the same modular group with its coordinates in two smaller groups.
:::

Left multiplication by $g$ takes every arrow $x\to xa$ to $gx\to(gx)a$, preserving the label. To transport a walk starting at $u$ to one starting at $v$, use $g=vu^{-1}$. In particular a Hamiltonian path in a Cayley graph gives a sequence of allowed operations visiting every state from any starting state. For a permutation puzzle viewed as a Cayley graph, that is a way to visit the solved state eventually. A breadth-first search tree rooted at the solved state, following reversed edges when needed, instead records shortest routes to it.

In a cyclic group $G=\langle g\rangle$ of order $n$, every subgroup is $\langle g^d\rangle$ for some divisor $d$ of $n$. Let $d$ be the smallest positive exponent with $g^d\in H$, which exists because $g^n=1$. Divide any exponent of a member of $H$ by $d$. The remainder also gives a member because $H$ is closed under multiplication by an inverse. Minimality forces the remainder to be zero. Apply this to the exponent $n$ to see that $d$ divides $n$. The order of the subgroup is $n/d$.

### Three periods for reducing an exponent

For a finite group $G$, its exponent is $\lambda_G=\operatorname{lcm}\{\operatorname{ord}(a):a\in G\}$. It is the least positive integer that returns every element to the identity, since it must be divisible by every element order. For any $a\in G$ we can reduce an exponent modulo $\operatorname{ord}(a)$, $\lambda_G$, or $|G|$. The first may be much smaller. For example, the group $\mathbb Z_{24}^*$ has eight elements, but every unit squares to $1$. Its exponent is $2$ and it cannot be generated by one element.

To find the order of a unit once a multiple $M$ of its order is known, test each prime divisor $q$ of $M$. If $a^{M/q}=1$, replace $M$ by $M/q$ and test again. When no prime factor can be removed, $M$ is the order. Indeed, any strict smaller divisor equal to the true order would divide at least one of the tested quotients. This test also recognizes generators: an element of a group of order $n$ generates if none of the powers $a^{n/q}$ for prime $q\mid n$ is the identity.

In a finite abelian group the exponent is attained by some element. For each prime power $p^e$ dividing the exponent exactly, choose an element whose order has this full prime power. Raise it to the part of its order coprime to $p$ to obtain an element of order $p^e$. The selected elements commute, their orders are pairwise coprime, and their product has order the product of those orders. For two commuting elements of coprime orders, a return $(ab)^k=1$ gives $a^k=b^{-k}$. This common member has order dividing both orders and is therefore the identity. So each order must divide $k$.

### Euler and Fermat turn large powers into small ones

Euler's theorem is Lagrange's theorem in $\mathbb Z_n^*$: for $\gcd(a,n)=1$, $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv_n1$. For prime $p$, $\varphi(p)=p-1$, giving Fermat's little theorem for $p\nmid a$. The all-integer version is $a^p\equiv_p a$ because the $p\mid a$ case is immediate. Look at which condition each version uses before reducing the exponent. For example, $4^{100}\equiv_7 4^4\equiv_7 4$. Here $100\bmod6=4$ and $4^3\equiv_7 1$.

When an exponent is still large after reduction, build $a,a^2,a^4,\ldots$ by squaring and reducing each time. Multiply the powers matching the binary expansion of the exponent. This works for all remainders and makes no assumption that they are units. Reducing the exponent by an order, by contrast, requires the group hypothesis.

In the arithmetic behind RSA, take distinct primes $p,q$ and choose positive integers $e,d$ with $ed\equiv_{\varphi(pq)}1$. The EEA gives $d$ when $e$ is coprime to $\varphi(pq)$. For a unit $x$, Euler gives $x^{ed}\equiv_{pq}x$. The conclusion also holds for a nonunit. Modulo either prime, the base is zero or Fermat applies because $ed-1$ is divisible by both $p-1$ and $q-1$. The two prime congruences combine to a congruence modulo $pq$. The powers undo each other because of the chosen exponent, which is the mathematical point of the construction.

### Reading non-abelian Cayley graphs and power towers

When a group $(G,\odot,1)$ is specified solely by a two-color right-multiplication Cayley graph $\Gamma(G,\{a,b\})$ with edges $x\xrightarrow{g}x\odot g$, order matters:

1. **Generator words via a spanning tree.** Trace short directed paths from $1$ to express elements as words in $\{a,b\}$ (and their backward-edge inverses $a^{-1},b^{-1}$).
2. **Products $x\odot y$ versus $y\odot x$.** If $y=g_1\odot\cdots\odot g_k$ along a path from $1$, start at vertex $x$ and follow that exact sequence of edge colors $g_1,\ldots,g_k$ to land at $x\odot y$. Reversing the roles starts at $y$ and follows $x$'s word to land at $y\odot x$. Finding a single pair with $x\odot y\ne y\odot x$ proves $G$ is **non-abelian** (and therefore **not cyclic**).
3. **Inverses $x^{-1}$ from the graph.** Find any directed path of colored edges $h_1,\ldots,h_m$ leading **from $x$ back to $1$**, so $x\odot(h_1\odot\cdots\odot h_m)=1$. Now start at $1$ and follow that **exact same color sequence** $h_1,\ldots,h_m$ forward: you land at $1\odot h_1\odot\cdots\odot h_m=x^{-1}$.

For **power towers** $a^{(b^c)}\bmod n$ with $\gcd(a,n)=1$, never reduce the exponent modulo $n$! Euler's theorem puts the exponent in the ring $\mathbb Z_{\varphi(n)}$ (or $\mathbb Z_{\lambda(n)}$): first compute $e=b^c\bmod\varphi(n)$, and then compute $a^e\bmod n$. Inside that upper step $b^c\bmod M$ ($M=\varphi(n)$), check $\gcd(b,M)$ before applying Euler a second time—if $\gcd(b,M)=1$, reduce $c$ modulo $\varphi(M)$; if $\gcd(b,M)>1$, factor $M$ into coprime prime powers and combine $b^c$ modulo each factor by the Chinese Remainder Theorem below. The same prime-by-prime split handles $(a^k+b^m)\bmod(pq)$ even when a base shares a prime factor with $pq$: evaluate modulo $p$ and modulo $q$ separately (using $0$ if the prime divides the base and Fermat's period $p-1$ otherwise), then merge the two remainders.

## 13.4 Homomorphisms and the Chinese Remainder Theorem

### Preserve the operation first

A homomorphism $h:G\to H$ satisfies $h(ab)=h(a)h(b)$ with the respective operations. We can multiply in $G$ and then map the answer, or map both inputs and multiply in $H$. Try that with $1_G\cdot1_G$. It gives $h(1_G)=h(1_G)^2$, and cancellation in $H$ leaves $h(1_G)=1_H$. Now $h(a)h(a^{-1})=h(1_G)$ shows that the map preserves inverses as well. It follows that it preserves integer powers.

The image is a subgroup because $h(a)h(b)^{-1}=h(ab^{-1})$ is again in the image. The same equality shows that if both inputs map to $1_H$, then $ab^{-1}$ does too. The set of elements mapping to $1_H$ is therefore a subgroup called the **kernel**. An injective map has only $1_G$ in its kernel. Conversely, if $h(a)=h(b)$, then $h(a^{-1}b)=1_H$. When the kernel contains only $1_G$, this forces $a^{-1}b=1_G$ and hence $a=b$.

A bijective homomorphism is an **isomorphism**, and its inverse also preserves the operation. The notation $G\cong H$ means such a map exists.

The map $k\mapsto g^k$ is a surjective homomorphism from $(\mathbb Z,+,0)$ to $\langle g\rangle$. When $g$ has finite order $m$, two integers map to the same element exactly when their difference is divisible by $m$. Restricting to the representatives in $\mathbb Z_m$ gives the isomorphism with the cyclic subgroup. This explains why equal-order cyclic groups are isomorphic. Equal orders alone do not imply isomorphism: $\mathbb Z_4$ has an element of order $4$, while every element of $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2$ has order at most $2$.

We can also evaluate words in group generators. For example send $a$ to $(1\,2\,3)$ and $b$ to $(1\,2)$, then evaluate concatenation as composition. In the prefix tree, $ab$ and $baa$ are different paths, but both evaluate to $(1\,3)$. Identify words with the same value under this map. Appending a letter multiplies each representative by the same element, so the next value depends only on the current class. This gives the Cayley graph as the quotient of the prefix tree: many histories, one resulting group element.

### Reconstruct a remainder from two coordinates

Suppose $\gcd(m,n)=1$. The Chinese remainder map
$$
x\longmapsto (x\bmod m,\ x\bmod n)
$$
from $\mathbb Z_{mn}$ to $\mathbb Z_m\times\mathbb Z_n$ preserves addition and multiplication. If two inputs have the same coordinates, their difference is divisible by $m$ and $n$, hence by $mn$. This gives injectivity, and both sides have $mn$ elements, so it is bijective. Units map to pairs of units and are exactly their preimages, because a prime divisor of $mn$ divides one of the two moduli. In particular $\mathbb Z_{mn}^*\cong\mathbb Z_m^*\times\mathbb Z_n^*$.

To reconstruct $x$ satisfying $x\equiv_m u$ and $x\equiv_n v$, find Bézout coefficients with $\alpha m+\beta n=1$. Look at the two terms separately. Modulo $m$, the first term is zero, so the second has to supply the whole $1$. Modulo $n$, their roles switch. Thus $\beta n\equiv_m1$ and $\beta n\equiv_n0$, while $\alpha m\equiv_m0$ and $\alpha m\equiv_n1$. So
$$
x\equiv_{mn}u\beta n+v\alpha m.
$$
Each term delivers one coordinate and vanishes in the other. For $m=3,n=7$, use $-2\cdot3+7=1$. The coordinate pair $(2,3)$ gives $2\cdot7+3(-6)=-4\equiv_{21}17$. Check $17\bmod3=2$ and $17\bmod7=3$. This construction can be iterated for pairwise coprime moduli. If the moduli share a divisor, first check compatibility. For example $x\equiv_4 1$ and $x\equiv_6 2$ ask for incompatible parities.

The same theorem explains $\mathbb Z_{21}^*\cong\mathbb Z_3^*\times\mathbb Z_7^*\cong\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_6$. The second factors are cyclic, since $2$ generates modulo $3$ and $3$ generates the six units modulo $7$. No element of the product has order $12$, since its two coordinate orders divide $2$ and $6$. So a product can give a quick reason why a group has no single generator.

The Carmichael function $\lambda(n)=\lambda_{\mathbb Z_n^*}$ is the least period shared by all unit powers. When $n=\prod_i p_i^{e_i}$, each prime-power coordinate must return to its identity. Thus $\lambda(n)=\operatorname{lcm}_i\lambda(p_i^{e_i})$. For example $\lambda(21)=\operatorname{lcm}(2,6)=6$ even though $\varphi(21)=12$. Finding a period for all units and finding an element that runs through all units are different tasks.

### Multi-factor CRT, modular square roots, and isomorphism tests

For three or more pairwise coprime moduli $m_1,\ldots,m_r$ with $N=m_1\cdots m_r$, either merge two congruences at a time or write the inverse isomorphism $h^{-1}\colon\mathbb Z_{m_1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb Z_{m_r}\to\mathbb Z_N$ directly using $N_i=N/m_i$ and $\delta_i=(N_i^{-1}\bmod m_i)$:
$$
h^{-1}(u_1,\ldots,u_r)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^r u_i\,\delta_i N_i\right)\bmod N.
$$
Because $h(x)=(x\bmod m_1,\ldots,x\bmod m_r)$ is a ring isomorphism (and restricts to a group isomorphism on units), polynomial congruences such as $x^2\equiv_N c$ split into independent coordinate equations $x_i^2\equiv_{m_i}c\bmod m_i$. If component $i$ has $s_i$ solutions, $\mathbb Z_N$ has $\prod_{i=1}^r s_i$ solutions! In any cyclic group $\langle g\rangle$ of **even** order $2t$ (such as $\mathbb Z_{p^e}^*$ for an odd prime $p$), $g^{2k}=1\iff 2t\mid 2k\iff t\mid k$, so $u^2=1$ has **exactly two** solutions ($1$ and $g^t=-1$). Thus $x^2\equiv_N 1$ in $\mathbb Z_N^*$ for a product of $r$ distinct odd prime powers has $2^r$ solutions, corresponding to every sign tuple $(\pm1,\ldots,\pm1)$.

When asked which groups in a list are isomorphic, filter invariants in order: (1) group order $|G|$, (2) commutativity, (3) cyclicity (all cyclic groups of the same order $m$ are isomorphic to $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m,0)$), (4) group exponent $\lambda_G$ (which separates $\mathbb Z_{15}^*\cong\mathbb Z_4\times\mathbb Z_2$ with $\lambda=4$ from $\mathbb Z_{24}^*\cong\mathbb Z_2^3$ with $\lambda=2$, even though both have order $8$), and (5) counts of elements of each order.

The Chinese remainder map changed how we wrote the elements while preserving what the operations do. We’ll ask a similar question about formulas in the next chapter. If we change the way a formula is built, how can we tell whether its truth value stayed the same on every assignment?
